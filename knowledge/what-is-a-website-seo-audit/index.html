What Is a Website SEO Audit

A structured review of crawl readiness, index signals, and content coverage—not a prediction of rankings.

Definition

A website SEO audit is a structured inspection of whether search systems can find, understand, and evaluate your pages honestly. It checks technical foundations such as crawl paths, status codes, and preferred URL signals, then reviews whether the content that matters for buyers is present, clear, and useful. It is not a promise of higher rankings, more traffic, or AI citations. Those outcomes depend on competition, demand, and factors outside any single report.

In practice, a useful audit produces evidence: sample URLs, severity labels, and next actions. A vanity dashboard that only shows a green score without explaining what broke—or what demand is still unanswered—is not enough for B2B teams that ship catalog changes often.

Why it matters

Search systems need crawlable links and useful, unique content to decide what a page is about. Public search-quality guidance emphasizes people-first content and clear discovery paths. When foundations fail, even strong product writing may never be evaluated fairly. When foundations work but topic coverage is thin, the site can look “healthy” while buyers searching for model fit, specs, or use cases still find nothing useful.

An audit matters because it separates those two stories. Teams that treat every issue as “SEO” tend to rewrite titles while crawl blocks remain, or publish more pages while duplicate URL signals fight each other. Separating foundation repair from coverage work keeps roadmaps honest.

How to check

  1. Define the audit scope: primary host, languages, and the templates that matter (product, category, documentation, blog).
  2. Confirm robots rules, sitemap coverage, and that important URLs return expected status codes without soft-404 traps.
  3. Sample crawl paths from navigation and internal links; note orphans and dead ends.
  4. Review canonical, hreflang, and duplicate URL clusters for conflicting preferred versions.
  5. Map buyer questions and commercial intents against live pages to spot coverage gaps—not only keyword lists.
  6. Group findings into health blockers versus growth opportunities, each with sample URLs and owners.

A concrete example

A manufacturer updates its product CMS. Titles look polished, and marketing ships new application pages. An audit still finds that the robots file blocks a documentation folder used in support links, several category hubs return mixed parameter URLs without a clear canonical, and three high-intent buyer questions only appear as PDF downloads not linked from HTML hubs. The audit output is not “SEO score 72.” It is a sequenced list: unblock documentation crawl paths, consolidate category URL signals, then build HTML answers for the unanswered buyer questions on the product URLs that should own them.

How Mika separates health issues from growth opportunities

Health issues are broken crawl, index, or preferred-URL signals—things that stop discovery or confuse which version should represent a page. Growth opportunities are topics, intents, or markets the site has not covered yet. Mika keeps those panels separate so a clean crawl is never mistaken for finished demand coverage, and a content idea list never hides foundation blockers.

FAQ

Is an SEO audit the same as a ranking report?

No. An audit reviews crawl readiness, index signals, and content coverage on your site. A ranking report tracks positions for keywords. Positions can move for reasons outside the site; an audit explains whether foundations and coverage are sound.

How often should we run a website SEO audit?

Run a full audit after major launches, migrations, or CMS changes, and on a regular cadence that matches how often the site changes. Between full audits, re-check high-severity health items such as crawl blocks and broken critical paths.

What should we do with audit findings first?

Fix blockers that stop discovery or confuse preferred URLs, then prioritize coverage gaps that match real buyer questions. Mixing every finding into one score hides which work is foundation repair and which work is growth coverage.
