Short answer

A result row shows a title link and a snippet, and the title element is the page’s own text for that link.

The screenshot is a results page for a query about an opera. Each row has a title link and text under it. This article does not treat the result count or the timing printed on that screenshot as a measurement, and it does not treat the order of those rows as a ranking of Mika or of a customer. The health question is whether your own title states the subject of your own URL.

Definition

The title element lives in the head. The H1 lives in the body. They should name the same subject, and they can differ in length without becoming two different products. A title that is only the company slogan on every URL makes every row look the same. A title stuffed with a city list is not a clearer subject. Search interfaces sometimes rewrite a title. That possibility is not a reason to leave the element empty or misleading, and it is not a promise that your wording will be kept.

What belongs in the page basics

Write a title that names the page’s subject, and keep the H1 on that same subject.

One title per document. Do not paste the homepage title into the product template. Look at the title in the fetched HTML, not only at a results screenshot you do not control. The opera results in the figure are an example of the row’s shape. They are not a target query and not a report.

What to check

  • A title element that names the subject
  • An H1 on the same subject
  • The homepage title not reused on every template
  • The title read from a fetch
  • No position copied from a third-party screenshot

A practical example

Every valve URL has the title “Home | Company” because the template never replaces it. The H1 is the valve family. The repair sets the title from the family name plus the company, and a fetch shows the new title element. Nobody records a position from the opera screenshot.

What this does not claim

A clear title does not guarantee rankings or citations.
