Direct answer

A question-led brief names the buyer question, the intent, the URL that will own the answer, and the sentence that must appear in HTML. SEO Growth uses that brief to close a gap. It does not assign a keyword quota.

Why it matters

Briefs fail when they list phrases and forget the constraint. The useful brief also says what the page must not claim, which document is evidence, and whether the job is an edit or a new URL. Near-duplicates are rejected in the brief, not after a dozen pages ship. Informational, commercial, and transactional intents stay labeled so the wrong template is not forced on the question.

What to check

Write the question as a person would ask it. Name the owner. Paste the required sentence, including the unit. Link the internal pages that must point at the owner. Schedule a re-audit that checks the live HTML, not the brief.

  • Start with the question, not a phrase list.
  • Name the owner URL and whether it is new.
  • Include the sentence with its unit and exclusion.
  • Plan the re-audit of live HTML.

A practical example

The brief says “target pumps.” The revision says the page must answer which pump suits a stated head, in one sentence, on the existing family URL, and must not add a city variant. That is a brief. It is not a rank order of phrases.

Content depth

A brief is deep enough when a writer can produce the answer without inventing a metric. Extra persona theater is optional. The sentence is not.

How an unanswered topic gets found

The brief should list the descriptive anchors to add. Publishing the URL into the sitemap before the sentence exists does not make it findable in a useful way. Re-audit after the links resolve.
